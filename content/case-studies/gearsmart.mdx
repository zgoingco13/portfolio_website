<Section title="The Problem">

**The trip that started it.** My first camping trip was Holcomb Valley in the summer of 2024. I relied on borrowed gear I didn't know how to use and underestimated the cold. By midnight my sleeping pad had completely deflated, and I woke up freezing with a headache. My friend Chris had made me a detailed packing list, and it still wasn't enough: a list tells you what to bring, not which items matter or how to use them.

**Why beginners show up underprepared.** Generic lists ignore the **real conditions** of where they're going. The **cost** of entry is intimidating and unranked. And there's endless gear content, but almost none **specific to their actual trip**. The result is overbuying, underbuying or giving up.

**How people choose.** Twelve secondary sources on the outdoor market and decision-making shaped three rules for the product:

- **Beginners buy cheap.** Most gear sold is mass-priced, and new participants don't start with premium gear.<sup className="ml-0.5 font-sans text-[0.7em]"><a href="#ref-6" className="no-underline">6</a>,<a href="#ref-8" className="no-underline">8</a></sup> So GearSmart treats budget as an input and defaults to affordable picks.
- **More options, worse choices.** Past a certain point, extra options lower satisfaction and raise regret,<sup className="ml-0.5 font-sans text-[0.7em]"><a href="#ref-9" className="no-underline">9</a>,<a href="#ref-13" className="no-underline">13</a></sup> and too many options on mobile leads people to give up.<sup className="ml-0.5 font-sans text-[0.7em]"><a href="#ref-10" className="no-underline">10</a></sup> So GearSmart makes one confident list instead of a catalog.
- **Gear is a belonging problem.** For women, cost, intimidation and gear itself all keep people out of a sport.<sup className="ml-0.5 font-sans text-[0.7em]"><a href="#ref-14" className="no-underline">14</a></sup> So every item explains *why* in plain language, framed around safety and confidence.

**The finding I didn't expect.** Mid-semester I presented GearSmart to Rhys Newman, an industry reviewer from my program's Interaction Design Advisory Board. He told me "beginner/intermediate/expert is too vague." When I ran three personas through v3, each one would have rated themselves higher than their skill on the trip they were planning, the same gap the Rocky Mountain study found in real hikers.<sup className="ml-0.5 font-sans text-[0.7em]"><a href="#ref-3" className="no-underline">3</a></sup> The real problem wasn't gear. It was **calibration**, so v4 asks a calibration question instead of trusting the self-rating.

**The reframe.** My first question, in March, was broad: how to present outdoor resources so people could prepare. Every round of research pointed at something narrower, the missing experienced friend.

<KeyInsight label="How might we">

*How might we digitally recreate the experienced friend who knows what's needed, so anyone can confidently pack and enjoy their time outdoors?*

GearSmart is that friend, for the people who don't have one.

</KeyInsight>

**Design moves.**

- **A schema, not a prompt.** I designed the structure the AI has to fill: 4 to 6 categories of 3 to 6 items, each with an Essential, Recommended or Optional priority, a reason scaled to how much the choice matters, and one top pick with a one-line why. The caps keep the list short enough to act on.
- **One question at a time.** Setup asks for the activity, then the trip, then experience, so each step narrows the context before any gear appears.
- **Detail on tap.** The list shows each item's name and priority first. The reason and product options open only when you tap, so the list stays short on a phone.

**Four versions.** V1 proved the idea: a trip in, a list out. V2 put Essential and Recommended badges on every item and added a progress bar. V3 redesigned each item to open into "why this item" and added a running shopping total. V4 added the calibration question, group and solo trips, and weather-source citations. Under the hood, I moved the API keys server-side and switched to structured tool use, so the list doesn't break on bad data.

**The pivot that defines it.** I applied to REI's affiliate program; they rejected it. So I rebuilt the entire purchase layer to be **retailer-agnostic**, searching trusted retailers and routing to whoever's cheapest.

**Designing in AR.** Fit is the other half of being prepared: the right pack, worn wrong, still hurts. The first time I tested the AR Fit Guide on a real pack, I learned an overlay can show you the target but can't confirm you've hit it, so v2 tracks the body, not the gear. It reads 17 body keypoints live in the browser, coaches you through five fitting steps, then confirms the fit and remembers your torso size for next time. The physical test taught me what the screen couldn't.

    <p><strong>It started with a bad night.</strong> On my first camping trip, at Holcomb Valley, my sleeping pad deflated by midnight and I woke up freezing. Chris's detailed packing list still wasn't enough: a list tells you what to bring, not why you need it or how to use it. That gap became the project.</p>
    <p><strong>The real problem wasn't gear.</strong> Beginners don't lack information; they lack information built for their trip. And people over-rate their own skill on exactly the trip where it matters most. I set out to build a gear list generator and ended up designing around how people judge themselves.</p>
    <p><strong>What I designed, and what AI built.</strong> AI let me build and test in days instead of weeks: Claude Code wrote most of the code from my specs, and Claude writes each list. The decisions were mine:</p>
    <ul className="list-disc space-y-2 pl-5">
      <li><strong>The research:</strong> my first trip, the REI floor, the LCC session, the gear tasting and the competitive analysis.</li>
      <li><strong>The design:</strong> the flow, the interface and the schema that shapes every list.</li>
      <li><strong>The judgment calls:</strong> the retailer-agnostic rebuild, the calibration question, moving keys server-side and cutting three features I'd already built.</li>
      <li><strong>The testing:</strong> real beginners, the four-table exhibit, and treating every AI output as a draft to check.</li>

<Section title="What's next">

Elísabet Elfa, a senior product designer at Patagonia, reviewed GearSmart on my capstone panel and gave me three ideas I want to build next.

- **Pack weight.** Show a running total as you check items off. Weight decides what kind of pack you need, from a light shoulder-carry to a full hip-belt pack with load lifters.
- **A post-trip check-in.** After the trip, mark what you actually used. The next list for a similar trip gets smarter, and so do you.
- **Lending.** A Worn Wear-style option to borrow gear from people nearby, so a first trip doesn't start with a big bill.
