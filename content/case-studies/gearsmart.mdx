<Section title="The Hook">

The first time I went camping, at Holcomb Valley, I hit the moment every beginner knows: not knowing what you don't know. Later, working the floor at REI and leading a women's climbing community, I kept meeting the same person — a capable beginner, underprepared, not for lack of trying, but because every gear resource was written for people who already know. GearSmart is the friend in the gear store, for the people who don't have one.

</Section>

<Section title="Problem">

Beginners show up underprepared for three reasons: generic lists ignore the **real conditions** of where they're going; the **cost** of entry is intimidating and unranked; and there's endless gear content but almost none **specific to their actual trip**. The result is overbuying, underbuying, or giving up.

</Section>

<Section title="Process">

**Research with real beginners.** I ran a session with the Ladies Climbing Coalition — real first-timers, not classmates — using an REI roleplay and an affinity board. Three themes surfaced:

<KeyInsight>

*"I had no idea desert nights were freezing — I showed up with one layer."*

**Condition Gaps.** Lists must surface hidden, counterintuitive conditions, not just gear categories.

</KeyInsight>

<KeyInsight>

*"I wished someone gave me a list specific to where I was actually going, not just hiking in general."*

**Specificity Gap.** Built around the exact trip — terrain, season, duration — not a generic archetype.

</KeyInsight>

<KeyInsight>

*"I didn't know what was actually essential vs. nice to have. Everything looked equally important."*

**Priority Blindness.** A clear safe-vs-upgrade signal so beginners know where to spend first.

</KeyInsight>

The deeper finding: every target user over-rated their own skill on exactly the trip where it mattered most. The real problem wasn't gear — it was **calibration**.

**Four versions, and a rebuild.** V1 worked but leaked API keys and broke on bad data. I moved every secret server-side, replaced fragile parsing with the AI's structured tool-use, added retries and spending caps — shipping to real users meant making it not-broken.

**The pivot that defines it.** I applied to REI's affiliate program; they rejected it. So I rebuilt the entire purchase layer to be **retailer-agnostic** — searching trusted retailers and routing to whoever's cheapest. The rejection made the product better.

**What I removed.** The strongest decisions were walk-backs — designed, tested, and pulled because simpler served the user better:

- **Per-person pricing.** Built it, tested it, removed it — splitting cost across people obscured the per-item decision the user actually needed to make.
- **Hover tooltips.** Designed for desktop power users, but the real flow was mobile and one-handed. Tooltips became a tap target nobody wanted.
- **Solo-mode toggle.** Solved a problem most users didn't have, at the cost of an extra decision every user *did* have to make.

<Section title="What I Designed vs. What AI Built">

AI built the prototype fast — that's the point; it let me test ideas in days, not weeks. What's mine is the thinking. The problem came from the REI floor and my own first trip, validated against real beginners before I built anything. The structure — every item carrying a *why*, tied to the actual forecast for your destination and dates — was a schema I designed and iterated across four versions. The biggest call was the retailer-agnostic pivot after REI's rejection — judgment AI doesn't hand you. And I tested with a real climbing community, not classmates, and changed the product based on what they did. The tool is the artifact; the judgment is the design.

</Section>

<Section title="Validation">

Tested with beginner climbers (LCC + remote), reviewed by a panel of working designers — including a senior product designer at Patagonia and a designer on Adobe's design-system team — and exhibited publicly through a four-table experience where visitors compared their own packing guess against a researched list: the gap between guessing and knowing, made physical.

<Section title="Reflection">

GearSmart taught me what AI is good for as a design material: speed. But the defensible work is the judgment underneath — framing the real problem, designing the reasoning structure, the retailer-agnostic pivot, and testing with real beginners instead of classmates. The tool is the artifact. The judgment is the design.
